Holly Miller
Dear Wolcott Cousins,

The Wolcott Family Society is ever changing and evolving. We have now gone to England for the first reunion ever to be held so far away from North America. I'm sorry that not everyone who wanted to go, was able to but maybe we can plan to go back in the future.

Barry and Ann George did a wonderful job of planning this entire trip. There were so many excellent tours and meals planned that it's hard to remember them all (good thing I saved the schedule!) Sleeping in a castle hotel was so special! The accommodations were luxurious, and the staff bent over backwards to be sure we had everything we needed.

My favorite memory of the entire trip was the Sunday morning church service. I walked up a hill on a small dirt road in the rain towards the steeple I could see in the distance. There was a little wooden sign "Tolland Church" pointing me to the left and then big stone and wrought iron gates proclaiming, "Enter into His gates with Thanksgiving!" During this walk the church bells began to peal joyfully. When 1 entered the warm stone church, five bell ringers were pulling on the ropes and continued to ring the bells for about 10 minutes. I had chills as I listened because these are the bells that the Wolcott Family Society donated and dedicated in loving memory of our past President Bill Wolcott several years ago. The church was packed with standing room only as the Bishop led the service. The organ and the voices filled the church with history and worship and love! During the annual meeting we also approved for the 2020 reunion to occur just outside Boston, and Deb(Enos) Adelson will be our host. We'll have lots more information about that in the future so stayed tuned and read your newsletters!

Taunton, England, Reunion Highlights

Ann and Barry George, Hosts
The 114th reunion of the Wolcott Family Society was held Wednesday, August 22 to Monday, August 27, 2018 in Somerset, England, with 38 people in attendance.

After all attendees had arrived safely and registered at The Castle Hotel in Taunton, some family members chose to retire early on Wednesday evening to overcome jet lag, while others met at The Pitcher and Piano for a lively dinner and chance to sample local Somerset ales and ciders.

Everyone was up early on Thursday morning to experience a full English breakfast buffet, including sautéed mushrooms and black pudding. The group had an orientation walk of Taunton through the High Street market and Vivary Park. A highlight was Bath Place, which had been "yarn bombed" to bring attention to the plight of bees in England. After a tour of the exceptional Museum of Somerset, some people visited St. Mary Magdalene church while the Society's officers held their meeting.

Thursday evening found us enjoying dinner in The Castle's private dining room, with local guests Chris and Mary Butler and Sharon and Dave Birt. Both couples were heavily involved in researching Wolcott family history during the last few months. After dinner, John B. Wolcott of Corvallis, Oregon spoke on the history of the Wolcott family in Somerset.

The journey on Friday in a private railcar on the West Somerset Steam Railway was a highlight for many. A visit to medieval Dunster Castle, where Wolcotts once lived and worked, was highly informative. After lunch in the picturesque village of Dunster, known for its yarn market, the group arrived in Minehead on the Bristol Channel for a short visit before returning to Taunton by rail.

Saturday was an exciting day as we bused to Henry Wolcott's birthplace, Watersmeet Farm. Chris and Mary Butler, along with their son, Daniel, welcomed us warmly, showing us the original tucking mill and opening their home for a tour. The group walked the ancient public footpath to Gaulden Manor, where the new owner, Mark Homewood, kindly permitted us to view the grounds. A brief stop in the hamlet of Tolland allowed for a look at two former Wolcott farmhouses, Jays Farm owned by Sharon and Dave Birt, as well as Kay Oxley's Woolcotts.

From Tolland, we journeyed to Brompton Regis in Exmoor National Park for a ploughman's lunch at The George Inn.Neighbouring this pub is the Church of St. Mary, a medieval church where Wolcotts once worshipped.

Following a lovely drive back to The Castle Hotel through the Somerset countryside, the reunion banquet was held in the private dining room, with many wearing tiaras, or even crowns, in a nod to the hotel's history. To open the subsequent business meeting, the anthems of England, the United States and Canada were sung. Bryon Kirwan, husband of Cyndee Wolcott Kirwan, delivered a highly amusing address to the Swine (Society of Wolcott In-Law Elites). Reports from the business meeting are available elsewhere in this publication.

Despite the sometimes heavy rain on Sunday, the service in Tolland's St. John the Baptist church was truly uplifting. Our group of 38 visitors combined with the local population to fill the church to capacity. The church was ablaze with light and flowers to honour both our visit and Margaret, the Vicar's, retirement service. Ruth, the Bishop of Taunton, led the church service and Wylie Johnson, a member of our group, did one of the readings. Holly Miller presented a cheque from the Wolcott Family Society for the preservation and upkeep of the church.

A short social at the church was followed by a hog roast at the Village Hall in Brompton Ralph. Again, we mingled with the Tolland locals and enjoyed drinks and a fine meal in their company before proceeding to Lydeard St. Lawrence's Church of St. Lawrence where Henry Wolcott and Elizabeth Saunders were married. Sunday was a truly extraordinary day because of the connection and camaraderie between the local community and visiting Wolcotts.

Monday started with a guided tour of the magnificent Wells Cathedral, the seat of religious power during the Wolcotts" time in Somerset. A drive through Cheddar Gorge took us to Axbridge, birthplace of John Wolcott of Watertown, Massachusetts. Lunch was enjoyed at The Oakhouse Inn, a pub in existence during the time Wolcotts lived in this village. John Walcott of Shropshire was our guest, but has no connection to our family's lineage.

Exploring Axbridge's St. John the Baptist church, located next to the site of Edward Wolcott's home and John Wolcott of Watertown's birthplace, gave us the chance to stretch our legs before touring the famous Cheddar caves. Upon returning to Taunton, our tired, but happy group shared one last drink at the Ring of Bells pub before retiring for the evening.

A final breakfast together on the Tuesday morning had us saying our good-byes, with promises to meet again next year in Charlottesville, Virginia, from August 2 to 5, 2019.

As hosts, we must extend our thanks to the wonderful people of Somerset and in particular, to those in Tolland who helped make this reunion so special. Maureen Canney, the church warden, as well as the Tolland Church Parish Council, including Neil and Caroline Longstreet and Beverlie and Tim Atkins organized the church service and added the extra touches such as the flower arrangements, the Canadian and American flags, and the social following the service.

Chris and Mary Butler, Sharon and Dave Birt, Kay Oxley, and Mark Homewood all graciously allowed us access to their homes to see some of the original features. As well, a special thanks must go to John B. Wolcott for his research and guidance on the history of the Wolcott family in Somerset.

Wolcott Locations in Somerset

Ann and Barry George, Hosts
1. Brompton Regis

It was once believed that the Wolcott family of Somerset were descendants of the Walcot family of Shropshire.
Later it appeared that the Somerset Wolcotts descended from the Woolcott family of Devonshire, which dates back to the 13th century. DNA evidence now makes both very unlikely. It now appears that they descended from a separate male ancestor who took his surname from a hamlet or property called Woolcott, in the parish of Brompton Regis, Somerset.
- Church of St. Mary - a 13" century church where Wolcotts once worshipped
- The George Inn is next to the church and serves lunch from Friday to Sunday
- Higher Woolcotts Farm has Woolcotts Cross Cottage for rent at www.woolcotts.com

2. Watersmeet Farm
The southern part of Gauldon Manor, containing two houses and a tucking mill operated by John Wolcott, was sold by George Mynne to Christopher Wolcott in 1618. It was inherited by Christopher's brother, Henry, who immigrated to America in 1630. The deed described it as "late parcell of Gauldon Manor", so Americans assumed, in error, that it was the house now called Gauldon Manor. The Wolcott land was actually the property now known as Watersmeet Farm.
- Watersmeet Farm is likely the birthplace of Henry Wolcott and is a privately owned farm requiring prior written permission to visit. Contact Chris Butler at chris@butler-harvey.com; he is generally welcoming to Wolcott visitors who have contacted him in advance.

3. Gaulden Manor
Wolcotts leased land from Gaulden Manor and later owned parcels sold off from the Manor, but none of our relatives occupied or lived at Gaulden Manor until long after we had come to America.
- Gaulden Manor has recently changed hands and is undergoing extensive renovations. It is privately owned and not open to the public, so please do not show up expecting to enter the property. The new owner has requested privacy.

4. Tolland
- Saint John the Baptist Church - a 13" century church in the hamlet of Tolland is considered a Wolcott family church and is open to the public. The Wolcott Family Society has helped fund it over the last 25 years. The Church Warden is Maureen Canneymaureencanney1@gmail.com. Please consider a generous donation to the church if you visit.

5. Dunster Castle
- seat of power and governance during the 1600s, heavily involved in the wool and cloth trade, had connections to the Wolcott family
- National Trust property open to the public, beautiful village as well

6. Axbridge
- Saint John the Baptist Church - a 13 century church with Wolcott graves
-The bank building to the left of the church steps is thought to be the site of the home of Edward Wolcott and birthplace of his son, John Wolcott of Waterstown, MA. When John left Axbridge for America in 1634, his father was the Mayor of the important cloth trading town of Axbridge.
- The Oakhouse Hotel, near the church in the town square, also existed at that time and is a great restaurant and accommodation spot.

7. Lydeard St. Lawrence
- Church of St. Lawrence - a 13 century church where Henry married Elizabeth.

Wolcott visitors: Please be respectful of private property and please leave a donation at each church visited as it is the only way they can remain operational.
- a reminder that most locations do not have bathroom facilities.
-if you have further questions please contact me at barry_george@telus.net

Tolland Wolcotts

Talk given by John B Wolcott at the 2018 reunion at Taunton, Somerset
In 1620, the people we Americans call the Pilgrims, got aboard the Mayflower and sailed to Massachusetts where they began what became the United States of America. Ten years later, in 1630, Henry Wolcott and his wife and three eldest sons left Tolland, Somerset, and sailed aboard the ship Mary and John to Massachusetts to begin a new life. With others they moved up the Connecticut River and established a new town called Windsor, Connecticut. The family prospered in Connecticut, where Henry and his eldest son, Henry, were Magistrates in the Connecticut Assembly.

In 1639, Henry's brother, John, wrote to inform him that their brother, Christopher, had died intestate, and that Henry was heir to his property in Tolland. Christopher was a successful textile merchant at Wellington, Somerset, and in 1616 had purchased, freehold, the property that his father, John Wolcott, had leased copyhold, which included two houses, and a mill which his father, and possibly his grandfather, had been operating as a grist mill. This is the property called "Watersmeet," now owned by Chris Butler, which we will visit on Saturday. Ownership of the property remained with Henry's son, Henry Jr., and his son, Henry. On the latter's death in 1709, the property was willed to his two married daughters.

The sons of Henry's brother, John, claimed that the inheritance should conform to the laws of primogeniture, and as males they should inherit the property. Two of them were married with families, so the youngest, Benjamin Wolcott, went to England to pursue his family's claim. In this he was unsuccessful, but at Tolland he found a wife. Benjamin married Joan, widow of Edward Galhampton, in March 1719 in the Tolland church that we will visit. Henry, son of Benjamin and Joan Wolcott was baptized there in November 1719. Both Benjamin and Joan both died in 1719 or 1720

Joan was Benjamin's fourth cousin, the only child of John Wolcott, a prosperous Tolland merchant. He left her the property in East Tolland called Burts, that we will visit Sunday. Joan previously married Edward Galhampton, with a marriage contract dated 1694, settling Edwards property at Stockland Bristol and Cannington on Joan for her lifetime. In 1695 Edward sued John Sydenham for not completing the purchase of land Edward had in Cannington. Sydenham said the property carried a legal impediment because Edward had signed a marriage jointure, settling the property on Joan Wolcott for her lifetime. Edward said he was buying other land which would replace it. The court agreed there was no impediment to the sale. The land Edward purchased in 1699 for this marriage settlement was the farm now called Gauldon Manor, that we will visit on Saturday. Edward Galhampton died in 1703, leaving his land in trust for his son, John, who died shortly after. His personal property was left to his wife, Joan.

In June 1720 administration of Joan's personal estate was granted to her daughter, Joan Winter, "during the minority of Henry Woolcott alias Galhampton, natural son of the deceased." Henry apparently died shortly after. Joan's heirs sold the Gauldon properties, and Joan Winter put the Burt properties in a trust.

Meanwhile, in America, Henry Wolcott's family thrived. His grandson, Roger Wolcott, became Governor of the Colony of Connecticut. Roger's son, Oliver, was also Governor of Connecticut, a Major General in the Revolutionary War, and a signer of the Declaration of Independence. His son, Oliver Wolcott, Jr. was also Governor of Connecticut and was George Washington's second Secretary of the Treasury. Another descendant, Roger Wolcott, was Governor of Massachusetts, and Edward Oliver Wolcott was a prominent U.S. Senator from Colorado.

With the encouragement of some other Wolcotts, Rev. Samuel Wolcott published a genealogy of the Wolco mily in 1886. In the process he communicated with many family members throughout the United States, resultir in the forming of the Wolcott Society in 1905.

My family joined the Society in 1945. Many of the leaders of the organization lived in Michigan where we lived, and had a picnic every summer. The Registrar was then an elderly lady, so my mother assisted her with writing letters, making up membership certificates, etc.. All the Wolcott Society records were kept in a trunk in our basement.

After finishing college and back packing through Europe, I visited England and went out to see Tolland. A bus from Taunton dropped me off at "Tolland Cross", on a dusty road in the middle of nowhere. I was able to find the church which was kept unlocked at the time. I then inquired about Gauldon Manor, which the Wolcotts, at that time, thought had belonged to Henry the immigrant. I was directed up the road and found the large house we will visit on Saturday. The owner, Mr. Cotterill, was in the yard, wearing knee high rubber boots and a kilt. He said he had just come in from the farm. He had heard of the Wolcotts, and was very hospitable. He showed me through the ground floor of the interesting old house, and some of the outbuildings. He was particularly proud of the old apple press formerly used for making cider.

That began my interest in Wolcott history. I joined the Society shortly after, and was Registrar of the organization from 1990 to 2014. I was co-editor with Charles Waid of the current Wolcott genealogy, published in 2002, and co-director with Charles Wolcott, of the Wolcott/Walcott DNA project begun in 2004. I also set up a Wolcott web page on the internet.

In the nineteen eighties, my wife and I were in London, and I brought her out to see Tolland. There was a sign in front of Gauldon Manor saying it was open on certain days for a fee. It wasn't one of those days, but we drove into the yard. Mr. Starkie came out and I identified our Wolcott connection. He allowed us to look inside the house and wander around the yard. The place had been greatly gentrified, compared to what I saw on my earlier visit. Marble floors had been laid and extensive gardens planted, including one with a dog's grave stone. There was also a small gift shop where Mr. Starkie offered to sell us postcards with pictures of Gauldon.

We returned to Tolland in the nineties and found the church locked up. By then I knew that my ancestors had lived at the house called Watersmeet Farm, so we bypassed Gauldon and went right to Watersmeet, where we were graciously shown through the house by the owner, Mrs. James. She showed us the huge Inglenook fireplaces, and where the hand pump for getting water used to be in the kitchen. We looked through the remaining half of the old mill, and speculated on how it may have operated. We'll see that on Saturday.

It was believed for a long time that the Somerset Wolcotts were descendants of the Walcot family of Shropshire. Gov. Oliver Wolcott, Jr., used their coat of arms on his personal seal for his correspondence. You will see this coat of arms in a window donated by an American Wolcott, in the Tolland church when we visit. There was no evidence to support this connection with the Shropshire Walcots, and our DNA project has proven it false. I later thought that we descended from the Woolcott family of Devonshire, which dates back to the 13th century. Again DNA evidence made this doubtful. The Somerset Wolcotts seem to be a separate family deriving its surname from a hamlet called Woolcott in the parish of Brompton Regis. In the 14th century the place name was spelled Wolfetecote, and Wolfcote. The last person with the surname of Wolfcote we have found record of was John Wolfcote living in 1463. There are now two houses at Woolcott, in Brompton Regis, called Higher and Lower Woolcott.

The first Wolcotts to appear at Tolland were on the 1524 Lay Subsidy Roll, where Thomas and William Wolcott are two of the eleven land holders in Tolland parish. Heads of households and individuals age 16 and over were taxed on either their land, goods, or labor, whichever was greatest; but those with less than IL per year were not taxed. One pound was the normal valuation of a farm laborer. Each individual was only taxed once even though he had property in more than one location. Thomas Wolcott was taxed on land at Tolland valued at 5 pounds per year with a tax of 2 shillings 6 pence, and William Wolcott on land at Tolland valued at 3 pounds per year with a tax of 18 pence. Most of the 1524 Somerset tax roll has never been published. I recently ordered the subsidy roll for Freemanors Hundred, which included Brompton Regis, Dulverton, Brompton Ralph, and other parishes. This document was only partially preserved, and Brompton Regis was unfortunately missing and Dulverton incomplete. I did, however, find three Wolcott families listed: John Wolcot of Kilton with land valued at 8L with a tax of 3 shillings; John Wolcott of Stogumber, for wages worth 20 shillings 6 pence taxed at 4 pence; and John Wolcot of Monksilver, for wages worth 20 shillings taxed at 4 pence. There were probably other Wolcotts in the missing pages and surrounding Hundreds, but this subsidy roll indicates that Wolcotts had been in the area for some time and had spread to nearby parishes by 1524. There was a Richard Wolcott with 7 children at Selworthy in 1560 and two Wolcott families with a total of 12 children at Brompton Ralph in 1569, none having a known relationship to the Tolland family.

Thomas and William Wolcott were on the 1524 and 1539 tax rolls, but Thomas was the only Wolcott at Tolland on the 1550 rolls. He was the father of four sons, Thomas, John, Henry and Roger. Henry was childless and Roger had only daughters, so Thomas and John contributed two branches of Tolland Wolcotts, branches that were united when Benjamin came from Connecticut to marry Joan. Both branches are represented here today. Both had lots of Johns and Henrys, so they are sometime difficult to keep straight. It may be helpful to remember that Thomas Jr. had a son John, the clothier, ancestor of John Wolcott who immigrated to Watertown, Massachusetts, in 1634, and Thomas' brother, John, had a son John the miller, ancestor of Henry Wolcott who immigrated to Windsor, Connecticut in 1630. John the clothier and John the miller were thus first cousins.

When the Wolcotts first arrived at Tolland, the parish consisted of two manors, Gauldon Manor owned by Taunton Priory, and Tolland Manor owned by Sir Richard Warre of Hestercombe. The present Tolland Road was probably the approximate dividing line. In 1520, Taunton Priory leased Gauldon with an adjoining tucking mill to John Selleck. The lease was renewed in 1580, and the Sellecks lived at Gauldon until about 1640. John's son, Robert Selleck, was uncle to Elizabeth Saunders, wife of Henry Wolcott, the immigrant.

None of the Sellecks were identified as tuckers, but Thomas Wolcott, Jr., is described as a tucker in his will of 1572, so apparently he, and possibly his father, Thomas, operated the Gauldon tucking mill. Tucking was part of the wool cloth making process, and was done using a water driven wheel. The tucking mill at Gauldon was still in operation in 1666. Based on this 1666 document we know that the tucking mill was located on the mill stream which ran between the Tolland rectory and what is now Watersmeet farm. I believe we now know its location. Gauldon Manor was taken from Taunton Priory by the Crown in 1539, and sold to William Standish in 1554. William was the uncle of Elizabeth Cross, whose daughter, Agnes, was the mother of Henry Wolcott, the immigrant. Neither William Standish or his heirs, the Mynnes, lived in Somerset, but William Standish's brother, Thomas Standish, was Rector of the nearby town of Elworthy. In 1567, Thomas Standish leased a house and land in Gauldon Manor to Thomas the tucker's son, John Wolcott and his son Henry. This house was probably located where the New Barn now is, between the tucking mill and Watersmeet Farm. John's will of 1589 describes him as a clothier. Henry Wolcott, brother of Thomas the tucker, was overseer of Thomas Standish's will in 1576, with John Wolcott as a witness.

William Standish's great-grandson, Robert Mynne, sold the larger part of the manor, then called Gauldon Farm, to John Turberville in 1615. The Selleck lease, however, didn't run out until about 1640 with the death of Robert Selleck. John Turberville, Jr. then had the Gauldon Farm house renovated, and the lavish plasterwork decorations installed. He and his family moved into the house about 1645. Gauldon was sold by John Turberville Jr. to Edward Galhampton and his wife Joan Wolcott in 1699. In 1720 Gauldon was sold by the Galhampton heirs to Richard Musgrove.

The southern part of Gauldon Manor, containing two houses and a grist mill, was sold as freehold property by Robert Mynne to Christopher Wolcott in 1618. The house and mill had been leased as copyhold property by Christopher's father, John Wolcott, son of Thomas the tucker's brother John. It was inherited by Christopher's brother, Henry, who immigrated to America in 1630. The deed described it as late parcell of Gauldon Manor," so Americans assumed, in error, that it was the house now called Gauldon Manor. Instead it was the house now known as Watersmeet Farm.

John, the clothier, son of Thomas the tucker, died in 1589, leaving a sizeable estate, including over 440 pounds to his eight younger children, and his property to his eldest son Henry, then 24. One of the younger children was Edward Wolcott, later mayor of Abridge, whose descendants, Barry George and Charles Wolcott are with us today. Henry would have taken over his father's 1567 lease of the house and land, which I think was at "New Barn" in Gauldon Manor.

Henry and his son, Henry Jr., were prosperous clothiers, buying and selling wool and wool cloth. The elder Henry's wife and son are buried in the large tomb in Tolland churchyard and he is probably in the now unmarked one next to it. His grandson, Henry, also left instructions to be buried in the tomb in Tolland churchyard.

In 1609 Henry leased by copyhold from the Warr family, owners of Tolland Manor to the north, a house and land at East Tolland, which we believe is the house now called Woolcotts. The property probably included considerable farm land to the north. Henry "surrendered" this land in 1624, probably to change the principal leasee. His son Henry, Jr., was the tenant there in 1638 when he purchased the property freehold, together with a cottage next door, occupied by one William Govier. The second house was called Whitemore, and apparently no longer

Henry Jr.'s brother, John, also leased by copyhold, from the Warrs, a house and property at Tolland called Greenway, in 1640. This may have been the house later called Burts, but that is uncertain. Both Henry and John Wolcott were jurors at manorial courts held by the Warrs at Chipleigh in 1626 and 1630. John Turberville purchased the Wolcott home at Greenway from the Warr heirs in 1654, and it became part of Gauldon Manor.

There is one other branch of the Somerset Wolcott family that deserves our attention. On Friday we will take the train to Minehead and visit nearby Dunster Castle.Dunster Castle was the property of the Luttrell family for several centuries. They were also lords of the Manor of Heathfield. Col. Alexander Luttrell took up residence in the castle in 1705 and died there in 1711. In 1708, for the nominal sum of one shilling a year, Alexander leased to William Wolcott, of Dunster Castle, yeoman, a house at Heathfield lately erected there by William's father Robert Wolcott, for the lives of William, his brother Robert, and their mother Agnes Wolcott. Robert was a great-grandson of Simon Wolcott, a younger brother of John Wolcott, the Tolland clothier. After Alexander Luttrell's death in 1711, the castle was run for 12 years by his widow, Dorothy, during the minority of her son, Alexander. In her will dated 1723 she said "I give unto William Woolcott twenty pounds in case he be living with me at the time of my decease to be paid unto him in twelve months after my decease." He appears to have had some administrative roll such as bailiff, with living quarters at the castle, for over 15 years.

In 1719, Dorothy leased to William's nephew, Alexander Wolcott of Heathfield, miller, a house at Heathfield called Whibbles for 8 pounds and, again, a minimal rent of one shilling a year. In 1731, Dorothy's second son Francis, who was living at Heathfield, took Isaac, son of Alexander Wolcott of Heathfield as his apprentice for seven years, providing food, housing, and clothing and all other needs, presumably to train for a position at Heathfield Manor like his great uncle held at Ludlow Castle.

Another Isaac Wolcott shows up at Dunster in 1712. The relationship is uncertain, but he was of an age to be another nephew of William. Isaac was a tailor in the town of Dunster, the first tailor in the Wolcott family. He may well have been the tailor that dressed the Dunster Castle Luttrells. Then, in 1716, Agnes Woolcott of Dunster married Francis Luttrell of Stogursey at Dunster. Assistant Registrar and DNA Project Manager's Report-2018

Charles William Wolcott
The Wolcott DNA Project continues to make progress toward our understanding of the genetic heritage of Wolcott and Walcott families in America and worldwide. Over the past year, fifteen new participants joined, bringing our total to one hundred and nine, including multiple surname spelling variants: Wolcott (61), Walcott (13), Wilcott (6), Woolcott (5), Willcutt (4), Walcot (3), Walcutt (2), Woollacott (2) and Willeuts, Willcutts, Wilcutt, Willeut and Wilkett (1 each). In addition, we now have participants with Wolcott DNA, but non-Wolcott surnames: Newberry (4), Pierce (2), Graves and Moore (1 each).

Some of the results received over the past year include:

• Walcot Family of Shropshire, England - We received DNA information from a second descendant of the Walcot family of Shropshire, England, confirming the lack of any genetic connection to the Wolcott family of Somerset. The DNA records of these Walcot participants fall within haplogroup (similar to a genetic ethnic group) R-P312, which generally is considered indicative of Celtic heritage, consistent with a family story placing the ancestry of this Walcot line among Welsh noblemen.

• Samuel Wolcott (1630-1687) of Shrewsbury, New Jersey - We received DNA information from participants with the Graves and Pierce surnames, proving descent from the Wolcott family of Somerset, England, through the branch including Samuel Wolcott of Shrewsbury, New Jersey. Our current hypothesis is that these lines result from one or more non-paternity events (NPEs) in which children with Wolcott DNA were adopted into families with the Graves and Pierce surnames. We are waiting on additional data which may indicate whether these NPEs occurred in America, as part of Samuel's travels in his trade as a mariner, or earlier in England, perhaps via Samuel's brother, Philip.

• Thomas Wolcott (179l-1825) of Oxford, Massachusetts - We received additional DNA information from a descendant of Thomas Wolcott of Oxford, Massachusetts, son of Moriah Shumway, an unmarried woman. This information proves descent from the prominent immigrant to America, Henry Wolcott (1578-1655) of Windsor, Connecticut, supporting Thomas' connection to his likely biological father, Thomas Freke Wolcott (1757- 1840). Descendants of this family were pleased to have definitive proof of Thomas' long-speculated upon connection to the Henry Wolcott line.

• Elijah Wolcott (1781-1840) of Litchfield, Pennsylvania - We received DNA information from a participant with the Moore surname, proving descent from the Wolcott family of Somerset, England, through the immigrant to America, John Wolcott (1599-1638) of Watertown, Massachusetts. This Moore participant appears to be the result of an NPE from a family with the Park surname of early Litchfield, Pennsylvania. Our current hypothesis is that a son of Elijah Wolcott of Litchfield fathered a child who was adopted into a closely related Park family of that community.

A Genetic Family Tree - Most people are familiar with traditional family trees, in which the nodes connecting branching family lines are identified with people's names. But there is an alternative genetic' family tree, in which the nodes are represented not by people's names, but by DNA mutations which, once occurring, are passed down to subsequent generations, connecting them to their common ancestors. Such DNA mutations are referred to as SPs (single nucleotide polymorphisms), representing specific mutations in the structure of DNA in which one of its four building-block nucleobases, adenine (A), cytosine (C), guanine (G), or thymine (T), is substituted by another.

It has been determined that SP mutations, on average, occur in any given family line only once every 125 years or so, or about every three or four generations. In other words, not everybody gets one! So, a genetic family tree based on SNP mutations will have less detail than a traditional family tree based on names. That said, DNA information provides an accurate, truthful record of the past. It is free from the errors often found in traditional family trees, and it is able to look past such human experiences as undisclosed adoptions, infidelity, out-of-wedlock births, surname changes and other ways in which people have sought to alter, conceal or otherwise overlook genetic truths. In addition, DNA information can reveal connections from the era before the days of established record keeping, or it can bridge periods where records were not kept, or did not survive. For every person alive today, DNA provides a clear, truthful record of their ancestry, free from any embellishments, concealments or other alterations. As has been said, "DNA does not lie".

SNP Branches of Somerset Wolcott Families - At the time of our 2017 report, we had determined that our Somerset Wolcott families were members of the Germanic haplogroup R-U106, which is believed to have separated from other Indo-European language groups in Central Europe about 5,000 years ago. We then have been able to track this family down a trail of SP mutations to Y19922, which is shared by all members of our Somerset Wolcott group tested for this SNP to date. Our current estimate for the age of Y19922 is 1425AD, or approximately a generation or two preceding Thomas Wolcott (1500-1555) of Tolland, Somerset, the common ancestor shared by all Wolcott lines in the Somerset group.

Downstream of Y 19922 our genetic family tree branches into distinctive SP markers identifying the principal (known) Somerset Wolcott lines. The first of these is Y20600, which is shared by all descendants of Henry Wolcott (1578-1655) of Windsor, Connecticut tested for this SP to date. In addition, descendants of the former missing link line of Samuel Wolcott of Boston, Massachusetts, share Y20600, proving their descent from the Henry Wolcott of Windsor line, and the downstream SP BY18842, providing an additional identifying genetic marker for this subset of the Henry Wolcott family. Newberry descendants of Josiah Wolcott Newberry (1779-1854) have also tested positive for Y20600. Our current hypothesis is that these lines are products of Samuel Wolcott (1679-1709) of Windsor, Connecticut, who graduated from Harvard in 1698, and reportedly died in 1709 without ever having married. That said, the genetic evidence points to this Samuel as the most likely candidate to have fathered children both in Boston while there as a student at Harvard, and then later in Connecticut upon his return to Windsor. As is well known, a man need not be married to father children!

The next NP branch downstream from Y 19922 is Y22158, shared by all descendants of John Wolcott (1599-1638) of Watertown, Massachusetts tested for this SNP to date. Included among these are many of the former "missing link" Wolcott lines including descendants of the frontiersman John Wolcott of Pennsylvania and related lines including Silas and Barnabas Wolcott of Pennsylvania, Abner Wilcott of Quebec, Joseph Wilcott of Delaware, Thomas Willcutts of North Carolina and Elijah Wilcutt of Kentucky, proving the close genetic connection among these lines.

Our third SNP branch downstream from Y19922 is Y29888, shared by descendants of Simon Woolcott (1558-1644) of Heathfield, Somerset and Jose Roberto Woolcott (1822- ) of Peru, demonstrating the close genetic connection between these lines.

Finally, we have identified downstream of Y19922 the SNP branch BY34177, shared by all descendants of Samuel Wolcott (1630-1687) of Shrewsbury, New Jersey tested for this SP to date. Downstream of BY34177 there is another branching, with BY34172 identified for descendants of Samuel's grandson, Benjamin Wolcott of Shrewsbury, and BY34174 shared by our Graves and Pierce participants.

With the development of these four 'brother' SNPs, Y20600, Y22158, Y29888 and BY34177, all descended from Y19922, we now have a means of determining definitively, via DNA testing, the placement of any future participants in our Wolcott DNA Project seeking to resolve their connection to one of these principal branches of the Somerset Wolcott family.

Non-Paternity Events - Revisited - When we began our Wolcott DNA Project in 2004, we were told by our DNA testing company, Family Tree DNA, to expect a 'non-paternity event' (NPE) rate of between 3% to 5%, representing cases in which a DNA record would not conform to an expected genealogical record. This estimate has held approximately true for participants in our Wolcott project. There are many causes for NEs, including infidelity, out-of-wedlock births, purposeful surname changes, and others, but the most common is adoption, in which a child is brought into a new family and given that family's surname, though carrying the different DNA of the birth father. Over time, knowledge of such adoptions may become lost to subsequent generations, as the purpose of most adoptions is to fully integrate the child into their new family, as demonstrated through the sharing of the family surname, rather than memorializing the status of the adopted child as 'the different one'.

Early on, however, we had an interesting observation... the NPE gate seemed only to 'swing one way'. We were able to identify several instances in which participants with a Wolcott surname carried non-Wolcott DNA, but we were not so able to do the reverse, meaning to determine, with certainty, that a person with a non-Wolcott surname was actually a Wolcott, genetically, based on their DNA. Perhaps such a person was merely a very distant cousin with a common ancestor in the thousand or so years preceding the adoption of surnames in Britain. Or it may have been that their similar DNA record was merely the result of coincidence. Determining that someone was not like you", genetically, proved to be much easier than that they actually were 'one of you'... with true Wolcott DNA.

The advent of SP testing has now resolved the issue of determining relatedness, meaning we can tell with certainty whether a person with a non-Wolcott surname carries Wolcott DNA. To date we have several such instances, including four participants with the Newberry surname who have tested positive for the SP Y20600, confirming descent from Henry Wolcott of Windsor, Connecticut. We also have one participant with the Graves surname and two with the Pierce surname who have tested positive for BY34177, confirming descent from the line including Samuel Wolcott of Shrewsbury, New Jersey. Our final participant in this group has the Moore surname, who has tested positive for Y22158, confirming descent from John Wolcott of Watertown, Massachusetts. Interestingly, this Moore participant may be our first double-PE, in that he is a Moore descended from a Park surname family, which itself carried Wolcott DNA. Going forward, we expect to identify an increasing number of people with non-Wolcott surnames carrying Wolcott DNA.

We remain encouraged that interest in the Wolcott DNA Program continues with a wide diversity of questions being addressed through DNA analysis. The field of genetic genealogy continues to evolve, with each year bringing new DNA programs promising previously unavailable insights into our collective genetic heritage. We look forward to continuing our efforts with the Wolcott DNA Project to advance our understanding of the origins and relationships among the early Wolcott and Walcott families in America and worldwide.
